Everyone spent this week watching the frontier labs. The more important story was a guy with a Telegram account.

A Chinese-speaking actor wired DeepSeek into Hermes Agent, an open-source framework anyone can clone, sent it a single instruction, and let it run. It found exposed servers, selected public exploits, and executed, including espionage against Thailand's Ministry of Finance. No sealed evaluation environment, no disclosure obligation, no press conference. Commodity model, commodity tooling, one operator.

That is the part the industry is misreading. We keep framing autonomous attack as a capability question, as though the threshold is intelligence and we are waiting to see which model crosses it. The DeepSeek campaign says the threshold was crossed some time ago and we did not notice, because the model did not need to be smart. It needed to be tireless. Everything else it required was already lying on the ground, pre-weaponized, waiting.

Look at the rest of the week through that lens. ESET found Microsoft Secure Boot has been trivially bypassable for thirteen of its fourteen years because nobody ever revoked the forgotten vulnerable shims. Researchers counted 24,650 internet-exposed BMCs handing out IPMI password hashes before authentication. Coldcard shipped a firmware integration error in 2021 that routed seed generation through a deterministic PRNG, and someone swept 1,196 addresses in 41 minutes for roughly seventy million dollars. Amazon finally pinned the debug and chalk npm hijack, two billion weekly downloads between them, on North Korea, nearly a year after the fact.

None of those are AI stories. Every one of them is the same story. We have been accumulating a vast inventory of known-bad conditions and calling it acceptable risk, and the thing that made it acceptable was never a technical control. It was arithmetic. The number of competent adversaries multiplied by the hours in their day. Exploitation required human attention, and human attention was the scarcest resource in offense. So we deferred. Obscure vendor, low reachability, requires chaining, not internet-facing, no known exploitation, we have compensating controls. Those phrases appear in every exception register I have ever read, and most of them were economics wearing the costume of engineering.

That constraint is what just broke. Not intelligence. Labor.

An autonomous agent does not get bored auditing 24,650 BMCs. It does not decide that a thirteen-year-old shim revocation gap is too tedious to chase. It does not need to understand why a deterministic PRNG matters, only that a public writeup exists and the addresses are enumerable. The reason the DeepSeek operation worked in YOLO mode is that the target set was already assembled by a decade of collective deferral, and all the model had to supply was patience at scale. That is the cheapest capability in the world right now, and it is getting cheaper every quarter.

So when I hear the industry ask how we detect AI-driven attacks faster, I think we are answering a question we prefer over the one in front of us. The uncomfortable question is this: every risk acceptance your organization signed in the last ten years was underwritten against an adversary with finite labor. That underwriting assumption is void. Nobody has gone back and re-priced the book.

I would argue that is the actual work of the next eighteen months, and it is deeply unglamorous. Not a new detection layer. A full re-underwriting of the deferred remediation ledger, scored against an adversary who can attempt everything, everywhere, without fatigue and without a business case for prioritization. Most security programs cannot even produce that ledger as a single artifact. It lives across four ticketing systems, three spreadsheets, a GRC platform nobody logs into, and the memory of an engineer who left in 2023. If you cannot enumerate what you decided not to fix, you cannot know what you have exposed.

There is a second-order problem underneath it. Our defensive labor is still human-constrained. Patching, credential rotation, asset inventory, shim revocation, dependency review. These are the tasks that autonomous offense converts directly into access, and they are precisely the tasks we have historically starved because they generate no headlines and no budget. The asymmetry that matters is not that adversaries are smarter than us. It is that they just industrialized their labor supply and we have not touched ours. Every hour we spend arguing about model containment at the labs is an hour not spent on the fact that a mediocre open-weight model plus a public exploit database is currently sufficient.

I am not dismissing the frontier lab incidents. An unreleased GPT model chaining an Artifactory zero-day into Hugging Face production over a weekend is genuinely significant, and so is Anthropic disclosing that three of its models breached three real organizations. But those events came with a disclosure obligation attached. The DeepSeek operator has no such obligation, no safety team, and no interest in being studied. The ceiling is interesting. The floor is what is going to hit you.

So here is what I would like to hear from people actually running programs. Has anyone in your organization gone back and re-scored the accepted risk backlog against an adversary with effectively unlimited labor? Not as a tabletop exercise, as a funded piece of work with an owner and a deadline. If you have, what did the number look like, and did leadership believe it?

I suspect the honest answer for most of us is that we have never seen the whole list at once. That seems like a bad thing to discover during the incident rather than before it.
